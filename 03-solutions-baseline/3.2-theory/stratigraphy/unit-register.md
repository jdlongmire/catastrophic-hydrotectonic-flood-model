# U1 — Coconino Sandstone

**The worked exemplar.** The only unit here resting on a retrieved open-access mainstream primary, and the one whose re-examination produced the restructure.

## 1. Location

Northern Arizona; outcrop belt ~200 km across. ~100 m thick at the Grand Canyon, at least 160 m near Ash Fork. Sits in the standard column between the Hermit Formation below and the Toroweap above — **carried as an address, and no step below uses it as evidence.**

**Not observables, kept out deliberately:** the Permian age, the ~10 Myr duration usually assigned, and any correlation to other "Permian eolian" sandstones. All are historical inference (rule 23a) and belong in step 3 as the rival's account, not here.

## 3. Problem statement

**The conventional interpretation is eolian** — a vast dry erg — and it is not a weak position. It has stood since McKee's 1934 work, it is the reading in the standard literature (Middleton, Elliott & Morales 2003 characterise the unit as *"fine-grained, well-sorted, and rounded quartz grains"*), and large-scale high-angle cross-bedding in a quartz-rich sheet sand is genuinely dune-like.

**The difficulty is that three of the measured observables do not match what that interpretation predicts.**

1. **Sorting.** Prolonged eolian transport winnows: wind is a low-concentration, highly selective medium, and mature ergs produce well-sorted sand. Measured sorting is 0.61–0.94 Φ SD, and Whitmore et al. state directly that *"the Coconino should not be characterized as 'well-sorted'."* The standard description and the measurement disagree.
2. **Roundness.** Long eolian saltation rounds grains. Measured grains are sub-angular to sub-rounded, and **K-feldspar is often more angular than the quartz around it despite being the softer mineral** — the opposite of what abrasion history predicts.
3. **Muscovite.** Platy mica is fragile under eolian abrasion. It is present in trace amounts in nearly every thin section throughout the formation.

**And the rival's own recent work concedes ground.** Brand & Maithel, publishing in a mainstream journal, propose **liquidization** for the deformation inventory, invoke **seismic events** as a trigger, and state that **"more water was present during Coconino SS deposition than previously recognized."**

**The rival at its strongest, which must be said in the same breath (rule 4a).** None of this refutes an eolian origin. Dune cross-beds can be preserved well below the 33–34° repose angle, so a 20° mean is not by itself decisive — and one published study argues the discriminating measure is the *spread* of dip inclinations rather than the mean, which cuts against the simple version of the dip argument. Damp or wet interdune environments are a recognised part of erg systems and can accommodate liquidization and even seismic triggering **without leaving the eolian framework at all**, which is precisely what Brand & Maithel themselves propose. Their conclusion is *more water than previously recognised*, not *deposited underwater*.

**So the honest problem statement is narrow:** the standard textural description of the Coconino appears to be wrong on measurement, and the interpretation built on it has not been correspondingly revised. That is a real problem about the *evidential basis* of the eolian reading. **It is not yet a demonstration that the unit is subaqueous.**

## 4. CHFM potential solution — *potential; requires discussion*

**Proposed:** the Coconino records **F2 peak translation** — sustained, high-concentration subaqueous transport over a moving bed, under the near-lithostatic pore-pressure regime the mechanism specifies. Walker axis: **Ascending**.

**What the mechanism explains that the erg reading strains on.** Under high-concentration mass transport, grains are supported en masse and are neither winnowed nor extensively abraded. **Poor sorting, sub-angular grains, anomalously angular K-feldspar, and surviving platy muscovite are the expected outcome, not anomalies to be accounted for.** The deformation inventory is consistent with elevated pore-fluid pressure in a saturated granular mass, which is the state the mechanism requires.

**Three things this does not establish, stated so the row cannot be read as stronger than it is.**

- **Scale mismatch on the deformation link.** Brand & Maithel's structures are centimetre-to-metre soft-sediment features in a surface sand body. The programme's Terzaghi collapse operates at a 15 km detachment in crystalline crust. Both involve elevated pore pressure; that is a **shared physical ingredient, not a shared event**, and treating it as convergent support was an overreach in the previous version of this file.
- **No paleocurrent data retrieved.** Coherent large-scale transport direction is the F2 criterion this unit most plausibly satisfies, and no specific dataset was obtained in this pass. **Named as outstanding retrieval.**
- **A wet-interdune eolian model may accommodate the same observables.** Until that is addressed on its own terms, this is a competing explanation and not a defeated one.

## 5. What would test it

**Currently: nothing. This unit forbids nothing and is `forbids: none` Tier 2 content.** Recorded as such rather than left ambiguous.

**Candidate tests, none run:**

- **Paleocurrent coherence at scale.** The mechanism predicts transport direction coherent across the whole outcrop belt and consistent with continental-scale translation. A wet erg predicts dune-field-scale coherence with regional wind-regime structure. **These differ, and the data exist** — this is the most reachable test on this unit.
- **The sorting prediction, stated before looking.** If high-concentration mass flow is the mechanism, sorting should degrade in a specifiable way with inferred flow concentration. That prediction has not been formulated.
- **Grain-surface microtexture.** Eolian and subaqueous transport leave different grain-surface signatures under SEM, and this is a standard technique with an existing literature. Not consulted here.

# ⚠ Flagged to JD — the restructure exposed a possible schema defect

**Surfaced, not acted on** (rule 11; schema revision is a belt revision reserved to JD).

**Is *"strong hydraulic sorting"* the right F2 criterion?** F2 is peak translation — hydroplaning at 9–40 cm/s over a moving bed. But well-sorted sediment is characteristically the product of **prolonged, low-concentration, repeated winnowing**, while **high-concentration mass flow characteristically sorts poorly.**

If that holds, the criterion points against its own mechanism. U1 is the demonstration: Whitmore's poor-sorting result is the strongest single datum *against* the eolian reading, and under the F2 criterion as currently written **it counts as failing F2.**

**Not changed here, for three reasons that survive whichever way it goes:**

1. Changing a criterion **after** watching a case fail it is the rule 1 violation this programme forbids, regardless of merit.
2. The belt already uses **"hydraulic sorting"** to name the *rival creationist* account in discriminator candidate 1's hypothesis set. Any change must resolve that equivocation or it makes the belt ambiguous in two places instead of one.
3. Sorting is a continuum. Mass-flow deposits are not uniformly poorly sorted — traction-dominated basal regimes can sort well. A blanket "catastrophic implies poorly sorted" would be as wrong as the current criterion may be.

**Recommendation if pursued:** its own package, stating what grain-size distribution the F2 mechanism predicts **from the physics, before looking at any formation.** That ordering is what separates a correction from an accommodation.
